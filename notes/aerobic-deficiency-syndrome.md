Over the past seven years I have been a pretty consistent, albeit lower volume endurance athlete. On average I recorded 350h of moving activity per year (6:43h/week). Mostly running and cycling. Also mostly unstructured and for fun. Performance is of course a broad spectrum. With races in the range of 1:25h half marathon (2022), 37:33 10k (2024) and an estimated FTP of ~300W (4.2 W/kg), I always thought my aerobic fitness should be alright.
The book "The Uphill Athlete" made me question that. Do I have the _Aerobic Deficiency Syndrome (ADS)_? And if so, should I care?

## Zones?

Basing the zones off the maximum HR is quite a wild guess on an individual. I have just blindly followed that and apparently should not have. Different zone categorizations exist, yet the key parameters seem to be

- AeT: Aerobic Threshold
- AnT: Anerobic Threshold

The 5 zone system found on Strava and Garmin follows 1 < 2 up to AeT < 3 < 4 up to AnT < 5.

### Where does my Z2 end? (AeT)

The Uphill Athlete focuses mostly around the AeT. It suggests a proxy, that one is below AeT when one can comfortably breathe through the nose. This really got me off-path into a rabbit hole. For two weeks I believed to be aerobically deficient. I won't be using the books non-medical "syndrome" term outside the title.

I can only breathe comfortably through my nose until about 135bpm. With my believed AeT at 170bpm (max HR around 205), this was extremely off. My drift test (run two identical loops of ~30mins below assumed AeT at a steady HR. if you are within 5%, you were below AeT) reaffirmed that. Yet, that was on me for accidently using my wrist HR and not realizing.

In the sections building up ones training plan I was also faced with a further indication of being aerobically insufficient

> Athletes in need of building a base: ​Have not averaged more than 400 hours per year of focused training for running or skimo during the past two years.

Their base phase implies no training above Z2.

What got me back on track, was a friend pushing back on my book-inspired wild claims. I did not feel like spending ~300 CHF for a one-time lactate test and was seeking for further ways to estimate my blood-lactate levels. With that I stumbled onto how heart rate variability (HRV) data could not only be tracked overnight, but also during activity. And that the same effects nervous systems work against each other during an activity. At higher intensities, the HRV is lower. I plan on writing more about this with an upcoming project. In short, this was finally a measurement that disagreed with my AeT being criminally low. With that I finally dug deeper and as part of that also realised that my drift test did not work with my external HR strap, but unreliable wrist data.

### Frustration with the book

This brings me to the criticism of the book. The usage of a medical term "syndrome" for something that is just a training-history related body adaptation feels icky and is probably a good overall example. I see the appeal from a marketing perspective. It's catchy. It suggests something is severely off and needs to be treated accordingly. This is simply wrong. I think it's fair to consider a regular Marathon an endurance event. It is run almost exclusively in a Z3/Z4 range. No performance-oriented marathon training plan I found suggests Z2 only. They all emphasize how important the adaptation to the continous leg beatings is and incorporate significant blocks above threshold. I am aware the trailruns can be substantially longer, but they certainly aren't all.

The book suggest training exclusively below AeT until AeT HR is within 10% of AnT. To me that seems highly wasteful for people with an already highly trained anerobic pathway. I see the argumentation to focusing the efforts in strenghtening the aerobic pathways. It also argued nicely how training one pathway comes at the cost of the other with the usage of capacity training and utilization training. It entirely left out that maintenance of a capability is significantly easier than building in up. To me, it again tries to make something complex simple. Unsurprisingly that new philosophy can be further "honed" through their coaching that is sold under similar name.

Besides the described frustration, I am grateful for the book helping me making my easy runs truly easy. The books overall methodology is probably more sound if you only intend on racing for over 6 hours. For me, 5k races (still 90% aerobic) and marathons are definitely part of my interest.

Overall, I am relieved I stopped following its methodology after two weeks. The realization that my AeT is effectively around 167 as the percentage of the max HR would suggest. 12% off my AnT. Througout the book I kept on having a key question unanswered:
If I am not recovery, but time-limited: Is it still better to train purely aerobically? Even in the "black hole" (slightly above AeT) the aerobic system should be fully trained, no?

The book [[Norwegian Singles Method Subthreshold Running Kept Simple]] had the runners time restriction as a core bit. It recognizes aerobic potential in most non-elites as well, but does not neglect sub-threshold efforts. I would definitely recommend that one more if you have a similar focus than me.

In short, don't fall for the cult like I initially did.
