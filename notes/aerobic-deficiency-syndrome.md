Over the past seven years I have been a pretty consistent, albeit lower-volume endurance athlete. On average I recorded 350h of moving activity per year (6:43h/week). Mostly running and cycling. Also mostly unstructured and for fun. Performance is of course a broad spectrum. With races in the range of a 1:25h half marathon (2022), a 37:33 10k (2024), and an estimated FTP of ~300W (4.2 W/kg), I always thought my aerobic fitness should be alright.
The book *Training for the Uphill Athlete* made me question that. Do I have _Aerobic Deficiency Syndrome (ADS)_? And if so, should I care?

## Zones?

Basing zones off maximum HR is quite a wild guess on an individual level. I had just blindly followed that and apparently should not have. Different zone categorizations exist, yet the key parameters seem to be:

- AeT: Aerobic Threshold
- AnT: Anaerobic Threshold

The 5-zone system found on Strava and Garmin follows 1 < 2 up to AeT < 3 < 4 up to AnT < 5.

### Where does my Z2 end? (AeT)

*The Uphill Athlete* focuses mostly around the AeT. It suggests a proxy that one is below AeT when one can comfortably breathe through the nose. This really got me off-path into a rabbit hole. For two weeks I believed myself to be aerobically deficient. I won't be using the book's non-medical "syndrome" term outside the title.

I can only breathe comfortably through my nose until about 135bpm. With my believed AeT at 170bpm (max HR around 205), this was extremely off. My drift test (run two identical loops of ~30mins below assumed AeT at a steady HR; if pace-to-HR drift stays within 5%, you were below AeT) reaffirmed that. Yet, that was on me for accidentally using my wrist HR and not realizing.

In the sections on building up one's training plan, I was also faced with a further indication of being aerobically insufficient:

> Athletes in need of building a base: Have not averaged more than 400 hours per year of focused training for running or skimo during the past two years.

Their base phase implies no training above Z2.

What got me back on track was a friend pushing back on my book-inspired wild claims. I did not feel like spending ~300 CHF for a one-time lactate test and was looking for other ways to estimate my blood-lactate levels. With that, I stumbled onto how heart rate variability (HRV) data can be tracked not only overnight, but also during activity—specifically using a metric called DFA $\alpha_1$ (detrended fluctuation analysis). As intensity increases, the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems work against each other, causing HRV (and DFA $\alpha_1$) to drop predictably across the aerobic and anaerobic thresholds. I plan on writing more about this in an upcoming project. In short, this was finally a measurement that disagreed with my AeT being criminally low. With that, I dug deeper and as part of that also realized that my drift test had not used my external HR strap, but unreliable wrist data.

### Frustration with the book

This brings me to my criticism of the book. The usage of a medical term like "syndrome" for something that is just a training-history-related body adaptation feels icky and is probably a good overall example of the book's tone. I see the appeal from a marketing perspective. It's catchy. It suggests something is severely off and needs to be treated accordingly. This is simply wrong. I think it's fair to consider a regular marathon an endurance event. It is run almost exclusively in a Z3/Z4 range. No performance-oriented marathon training plan I found suggests Z2 only. They all emphasize how important the adaptation to the continuous leg beating is and incorporate significant blocks above threshold. I am aware that trail runs can be substantially longer, but they certainly aren't all.

The book suggests training exclusively below AeT until AeT HR is within 10% of AnT. To me, that seems highly wasteful for people with an already well-trained anaerobic pathway. I see the argument for focusing efforts on strengthening the aerobic pathways. It also argues nicely how training one pathway comes at the cost of the other through the concepts of capacity training and utilization training. However, it entirely leaves out that maintaining a capability is significantly easier than building it up. To me, it again tries to make something complex simple. Unsurprisingly, that philosophy can be further "honed" through their coaching sold under a similar name.

Besides the described frustration, I am grateful to the book for helping me make my easy runs truly easy. The book's overall methodology is probably more sound if you only intend on racing for over 6 hours. For me, 5k races (still ~90% aerobic) and marathons are definitely part of my interest.

Overall, I am relieved I stopped following its methodology after two weeks, especially after realizing that my AeT is effectively around 167bpm—right where a percentage of max HR would suggest, and ~12% off my AnT. Throughout the book, I kept having one key question left unanswered:
If I am not recovery-limited, but time-limited, is it still better to train purely aerobically? Even in the "black hole" (slightly above AeT), the aerobic system is still being heavily trained, no?

The book [Norwegian Singles Method: Subthreshold Running Kept Simple](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/239471190-norwegian-singles-method) treats the runner's time restriction as a core constraint. It recognizes the untapped aerobic potential in most non-elites as well, but does not neglect sub-threshold efforts. I would definitely recommend that one instead if you have a similar focus to mine.

In short, don't fall for the cult like I initially did.
